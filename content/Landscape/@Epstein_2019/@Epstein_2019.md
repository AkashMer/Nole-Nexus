After reading <a href="Landscape/@Grieves_2016/@Grieves_2016" class="wikilink">the representation of space in the brain</a>, one of the biggest points of tension for me was the role of neocortex in spatial perception. This was because our ability to interact with space goes beyond navigation on a large scale. Such abilities are quick but precise, suggesting involvement of computational capacities of the neocortex. A constrained search brought me to this review paper and made few things clear:

- the research of spatial perception lives in clusters like the one here dealing with scene perception, earlier one dealing with navigation
- there are clear domain words which differentiate between *vista space* (limited within the sensory horizon) and *environmental space* (extending beyond the sensory horizon)

## Current landscape of scene network research

<img src="scene_network_state_dark.svg" class="wikilink" alt="scene_network_state_dark.svg" />\
([Amunts et al., 2020](#ref-Amunts_2020); [Forschungszentrum Jülich, INM-1, BDA, n.d.](#ref-siibra_2026))\
*On the surface, this paper maps three scene-selective regions. But at the core, it’s really about the simultaneous yet subtly different sensitivity of these areas to low-level features and higher-order processes, allowing probing into the mechanisms of scene recognition and spatial perception.*\
The locations of **Parahippocampal Place Area** (PPA), **Occipital Place Area** (OPA), **Retrosplenial Complex** (RSC) / **Medial Place Area** (MPA) envelop the V1/V2 areas of the visual cortex which may point to a possible evolutionary role of these areas. This is especially true for PPA and RSC/MPA which are located near the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus respectively allowing a connection to these phylogenetically preserved areas with spatially-modulated cells (<a href="Landscape/@Grieves_2016/@Grieves_2016" class="wikilink">The representation of space in the brain</a>).\
I chose to capture the complex processing of these scene-selective areas by showing their sensitivities to both low-level features and higher-order processes. The overlap is distinct at the bottom, but they share similarities even in the top processes for eg., spatial layout, local scene elements and spatial mnemonic memory all require delineating the overall structure of the scene. This implies that the differences between the regions are not absolute.\
Epstein & Baker go beyond just describing the current landscape and try to prompt future directions for research in the area including connections to the broader brain networks. This is captured by dashed lines in this bottom-up view of the functionality of the scene-network areas.

## Future directions for scene network research

<img src="scene_research_future_dark.svg" class="wikilink" alt="mindmap" />\
Some of the open questions for me which will direct my next reading are,

- Does the peripheral pRF (population receptive field) bias of scene-selective regions support scene monitoring in these areas, probably mediated by the relatively higher concentration of rods (sensitive to motion) in the periphery?
- The retinotopic inversion at calcarine sulcus matches the locations of PPA and OPA’s vertical visual field biases. Could this point to a developmental reason for their locations?
- Does the proximity of OPA to IPS (interface between perceptive and motor systems) and its adaptive nature to manipulation of the spatial structure of the scene suggest a role in action-perception coupling?

## Connecting scene network to other spatially modulated areas

<img src="outside_scene_network_dark.svg" class="wikilink" alt="mindmap" />\
My medical knowledge has always attributed hippocampus as a storage and retrieval system. My reading arc has softened that stance. I now believe this phylogenetically preserved structure has a more important role to play. How does the entorhinal-hippocampal system and the neocortex work together to build a sense of space?

##### One of the central concerns in this paper involved the relationship between low-level features and the higher-level scene information in scene-selective areas. Can this relationship and the hierarchical nature of these areas be demonstrated using RSA?

> [!synthesis]
> **Claim**: Scene-selective regions PPA, RSC/MPA, and OPA are sensitive to both low-level visual features and higher-order stimulus properties, mediating scene recognition and spatial navigation through overlapping processing with no absolute differences. Computational models, ecological validity, and broader network interactions are some of the open questions.
>
> **Contribution**: Epstein & Baker assemble a comprehensive functional map of the classical scene network in humans exposing connections to primate and rodent neurophysiology including hippocampus.
